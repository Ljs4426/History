Lesson 55 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

A Shift to the Right Under Reagan

How did the Reagan Revolution impact the nation?

Three Examples
01 · 1981

Economic Recovery Tax Act (ERTA)

Reagan's 1981 tax cuts dropped the top rate from 70% all the way to 28% by 1986. Supply-side economics: cut taxes on rich people, they invest more, benefits trickle down to everyone. GDP grew and the stock market roughly doubled over his presidency.

Other Perspective Supporters say the growth proves it worked. Critics point out the top 1%'s income share started climbing sharply in the early 80s and never stopped, so most of the gains went to people who were already rich.
02 · 1981–1989

Deregulation: Banking, Airlines, and Broadcasting

Reagan loosened rules on banks, airlines, and broadcasting. Short-term: cheaper flights, more TV channels. Long-term: savings and loan banks started gambling deposits on risky investments.

Other Perspective Over 1,000 savings and loans collapsed. Taxpayer bailout: $124 billion. Supporters say deregulation made the economy more competitive. Critics say it caused exactly the crisis it was supposed to prevent, and regular people paid for it.
03 · 1981–1989

Military Buildup and a Tripling National Debt

Defense spending nearly doubled: $158B in 1981 to $300B by 1989. Cut taxes + spend more on the military + don't cut anything else = the national debt tripled: $900 billion to $2.8 trillion.

Other Perspective Conservatives say the buildup pressured the Soviets into collapse and it was worth it. Critics note that running massive peacetime deficits became normal after Reagan, and the U.S. hasn't run a sustained surplus since.
Modern Connection: ICED Method

The Tax Policy Center (Urban Institute + Brookings) tracks how Reagan's tax changes affected income distribution. Their data shows the top 1%'s share of national income roughly doubled between 1980 and 2020 ("Historical Highest Marginal Income Tax Rates," Tax Policy Center, 2023). Cutting the top rate from 70% to 28% meant people with the most capital kept the most gains. Deregulation made that worse for anyone outside the financial sector. Current debates about taxing the wealthy and the wealth gap all trace back to those years. The baseline for what counts as a "normal" tax rate got set in 1981 and hasn't really shifted.

Importance & Impact

Reagan didn't just change policy, he changed what counted as normal. Tax cuts, deregulation, and big deficits all became politically acceptable after him, for both parties. Democrats and Republicans spent the next 40 years working inside the same assumptions. You can't make sense of modern arguments about taxes or the national debt without knowing what happened in 1981.

Ending the Cold War

What were the effects of Ronald Reagan's and George H. W. Bush's foreign policy actions?

Three Examples
01 · 1981–1987
Reagan Doctrine + Strategic Defense Initiative

Rejecting Détente: The Reagan Doctrine and "Star Wars"

Reagan rejected détente and went on offense. He funded anti-communist rebels in Nicaragua, Afghanistan, and Angola (the Reagan Doctrine) and pushed SDI (a space-based missile shield that didn't actually work yet) as a threat to force an arms race the Soviets couldn't afford.

Other Perspective One side credits Reagan's pressure with forcing the Soviet collapse. The other says Gorbachev's reforms and internal Soviet decay were already doing that. Reagan just happened to be president when it fell.
02 · 1985–1988
Geneva · Reykjavik · Washington · Moscow

Reagan-Gorbachev Summits and the INF Treaty

Reagan and Gorbachev met four times between 1985 and 1988: Geneva, Reykjavik, Washington, Moscow. The result was the INF Treaty in 1987, the first arms deal to actually eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons instead of just capping them. Over 2,600 missiles destroyed.

Other Perspective Reykjavik almost produced a deal to eliminate all nuclear weapons, but it fell apart over SDI. The INF Treaty held for 30 years until the U.S. pulled out in 2019, restarting the arms competition it had paused.
03 · 1989–1991
Nov 9, 1989 · Dec 25, 1991

The Berlin Wall Falls and the USSR Dissolves

November 9, 1989: East Germany opened its borders, crowds tore down the Berlin Wall. December 25, 1991: the Soviet Union dissolved into 15 countries. Bush Sr. deliberately kept quiet (no victory speeches, no gloating) to avoid cornering Gorbachev and triggering a hardliner backlash that could've reversed everything.

Other Perspective Some Americans wanted a bigger celebration. Most historians now say the restraint was the right call. Humiliating Russia had consequences. The lack of that restraint in later years shaped everything that followed.
Modern Connection: ICED Method

Foreign policy scholar John Mearsheimer has argued that NATO's expansion eastward after the Cold War set up the current conflict with Russia. In a 2022 essay in Foreign Affairs, he wrote that U.S. policy after 1991 "ignored Russia's bright red lines" by pushing Ukraine toward NATO membership (Mearsheimer, "The Causes and Consequences of the Ukraine War," Foreign Affairs, 2022). You can disagree with his argument, but the expansion decisions (Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic in 1999, then the Baltic states in 2004) built the security situation Russia has been reacting to. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine is evidence the Cold War didn't end, it just went quiet for a while. The disputes over who won and what that meant are still going.

Importance & Impact

The Cold War ended messy. NATO gained 14 new members. Nuclear arsenals never went to zero. The INF Treaty died in 2019. The U.S. became the world's only superpower and started showing up militarily everywhere: Iraq, Afghanistan, the Pacific. Every major foreign policy debate of the past 30 years traces back to how 1991 played out.

Moving Forward: Debating America's Founding Ideals

How does our nation define and debate progress as we work to preserve American ideals?

Three Examples
01 · Securing Liberty
First Amendment · Student Speech

Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L. (2021)

Decided
June 2021
Vote
8 – 1
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Justice Breyer

A Pennsylvania cheerleader didn't make varsity, posted a profanity-filled Snapchat from a gas station on a Saturday, and got suspended from the team. The ACLU sued. Supreme Court ruled 8-1 that her First Amendment rights were violated, making it the first ruling ever on off-campus, online student speech.

What It Settled (and Didn't) Schools still have some authority over off-campus speech (cyberbullying, cheating, that kind of thing). But a private post from home outside school hours is substantially protected now. The Tinker armband became a Snapchat. Tactic Used Strategic ACLU litigation building on 50+ years of Tinker precedent, framed as a free speech chilling-effect case.
02 · Safeguarding Equality
Title VII · Employment · LGBTQ+ Rights

Bostock v. Clayton County, Georgia (2020)

Decided
June 2020
Vote
6 – 3
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Justice Gorsuch

Title VII (1964) bans employment discrimination "because of sex." The Court ruled 6-3 in Bostock that this also covers sexual orientation and gender identity. The majority opinion came from Gorsuch (a Trump appointee) using a straight textual reading of "because of sex."

Why It Mattered Before Bostock, LGBTQ+ workers in over half of U.S. states had no federal employment protection. After: they did, through a 60-year-old law interpreted differently. Expanding rights doesn't always need Congress. Sometimes courts just reread what's already there. Tactic Used Strategic case consolidation: three separate cases combined to maximize the chance of one unified national ruling.
03 · Defending Democracy
Redistricting · Gerrymandering · Voting

Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) and the State-Level Response

Decided
June 2019
Vote
5 – 4
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Chief Justice Roberts

The Court ruled 5-4 in Rucho that federal courts can't hear partisan gerrymandering cases; it's a "political question" outside their reach. States can draw maps to lock in one party's power and federal courts can't stop them. But state courts still can.

What Happened Next Since 2019, advocacy groups shifted to state courts, ballot initiatives, and independent redistricting commissions. Michigan, Colorado, Arizona, California all made moves. When one door closes, the fight moves to another one. Tactic Used Venue-shifting: from federal courts to state courts and ballot initiatives after the federal door closed.
Modern Connection: ICED Method

In 2021 the Supreme Court ruled on off-campus student social media speech for the first time in Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L., a case that started with a cheerleader's Snapchat from a gas station on a Saturday. Writing for an 8-1 majority, Justice Breyer held that schools have "diminished" authority over off-campus speech and that punishing the post violated the First Amendment (Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L., 594 U.S. ___, 2021). The ruling extended the 1969 Tinker framework (that students don't "shed their constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate") into the social media era. That's the case that covers what you can post on Snapchat or TikTok without your school punishing you. Defending liberty doesn't happen once; it gets re-litigated in every generation's medium.

Importance & Impact

Constitutional rights don't enforce themselves. Every generation fights for them through whatever channels are still open: courts, ballot measures, state legislatures. The tactics change (Snapchat instead of armbands, redistricting commissions instead of federal lawsuits) but the work is the same. These three cases from 2019–2021 are just the latest round.
