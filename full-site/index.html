Lesson 55 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

A Shift to the Right Under Reagan

How did the Reagan Revolution impact the nation?

Three Examples
01 · 1981

Economic Recovery Tax Act (ERTA)

The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 slashed the top marginal income tax rate from 70% to 50%, eventually all the way down to 28% by 1986. The theory was supply-side economics — cut taxes on top earners and they'll invest more, which creates jobs and growth that "trickle down" to everyone else. Real GDP grew at roughly 3.5% annually during Reagan's second term and the S&P 500 rose about 113% over his presidency.

POV Tension Supporters point to economic growth and job creation as proof the cuts worked. Critics note that the share of national income going to the top 1% began its sharp upward climb in the early 1980s and never reversed — meaning the growth went disproportionately to people who were already wealthy.
02 · 1981–1989

Deregulation: Banking, Airlines, and Broadcasting

Reagan accelerated deregulation across multiple sectors. Banking deregulation allowed savings and loan institutions to take on far riskier investments. Airlines were already deregulating under Carter, but Reagan pushed further in finance and broadcasting. Short-term, consumers got cheaper airfares and more TV channels. Long-term, the consequences were more complicated.

POV Tension The savings and loan crisis of the late 1980s collapsed over 1,000 financial institutions — the federal bailout cost taxpayers an estimated $124 billion. Supporters say deregulation created a more dynamic economy. Critics say it created exactly the conditions for crisis it was supposed to prevent, and the bill went to ordinary Americans.
03 · 1981–1989

Military Buildup and a Tripling National Debt

Reagan dramatically increased defense spending — from $158 billion in 1981 to over $300 billion by 1989. Combined with tax cuts that reduced revenue and no significant cuts elsewhere, the national debt nearly tripled: from roughly $900 billion in 1980 to $2.8 trillion by 1989.

POV Tension Conservatives argue the military buildup pressured the Soviet Union and helped win the Cold War. Critics point out that the political acceptability of large peacetime deficits, normalized in the 1980s, became permanent. The U.S. has not run a sustained surplus since.
Modern Connection — ICED Method
I

The Tax Policy Center, a nonpartisan research group run by the Urban Institute and the Brookings Institution, tracks the long-term distributional effects of U.S. tax policy changes going back to the Reagan era.

C

According to their analysis, the share of income going to the top 1% of American households roughly doubled between 1980 and 2020 ("Historical Highest Marginal Income Tax Rates," Tax Policy Center, 2023).

E

This is the direct downstream effect of cutting the top marginal rate from 70% to 28% over Reagan's two terms, combined with deregulation that increased returns for financial-sector investors.

D

Today, every argument about whether to raise taxes on top earners, regulate Wall Street, or close the wealth gap is a direct continuation of the framework Reagan set in 1981. The debate's starting point was reset in those years and hasn't fully moved since.

Importance & Impact

The Reagan Revolution didn't just change policy — it reset the defaults of American economics. Tax cuts as a primary tool, deregulation as a default instinct, and large peacetime deficits as politically tolerable all became bipartisan habits after 1989. Every modern debate about marginal tax rates, financial regulation, and the national debt is a continuation of arguments that started in 1981.

Lesson 56 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

Ending the Cold War

What were the effects of Ronald Reagan's and George H. W. Bush's foreign policy actions?

Three Examples
01 · 1981–1987
Reagan Doctrine + SDI

Rejecting Détente: The Reagan Doctrine and "Star Wars"

Reagan rejected the détente strategy of Nixon and Carter. His approach: support anti-communist fighters in Nicaragua, Afghanistan, and Angola through covert funding (the Reagan Doctrine), while pushing an arms buildup the Soviets couldn't match. The Strategic Defense Initiative — a proposed space-based missile shield — was technically unworkable at the time, but the threat of an arms race the USSR couldn't afford was real economic pressure.

POV Tension One school credits Reagan's pressure with forcing the Soviet economic collapse. Another argues Gorbachev's own reform program, internal Soviet decay, and nationalist movements in Eastern Europe were the real causes — and Reagan just got credit for a collapse already happening.
02 · 1985–1988
Geneva · Reykjavik · Washington · Moscow

Reagan-Gorbachev Summits and the INF Treaty

Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Gorbachev held four summits between 1985 and 1988. The result was the INF Treaty in 1987 — the first arms control agreement to actually eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons rather than just cap them. It destroyed over 2,600 missiles from both sides.

POV Tension The INF Treaty held for three decades before the Trump administration withdrew in 2019, citing Russian violations, restarting the arms competition the treaty had paused.
03 · 1989–1991
Nov 9, 1989 · Dec 25, 1991

The Berlin Wall Falls and the USSR Dissolves

On November 9, 1989, East Germany opened its borders and crowds began tearing down the Berlin Wall. Two years later, on December 25, 1991, the Soviet Union formally dissolved. President Bush deliberately played a low-key role — no triumphal speeches — to avoid humiliating Gorbachev and triggering a hardliner coup that might have reversed the whole thing.

POV Tension Bush's restraint was criticized at the time as too passive. Most historians now credit the quiet diplomacy as strategically correct — a humiliated Russia lashing back was a real risk, and that restraint shaped everything that followed.
Modern Connection — ICED Method
I

Foreign policy scholar John Mearsheimer has argued for over a decade that NATO's post-Cold War expansion eastward was the fundamental driver of current tensions between Russia and the West.

C

In a 2022 essay in Foreign Affairs, Mearsheimer wrote that U.S. policy after 1991 "ignored Russia's bright red lines" by encouraging Ukraine's aspirations to join NATO (Mearsheimer, "The Causes and Consequences of the Ukraine War," Foreign Affairs, 2022).

E

Whether or not you accept his full thesis, the choices Bush Sr. and his successors made about NATO between 1989 and 1999 set the security architecture that Russia has been reacting against ever since.

D

The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 and the ongoing war demonstrate that the Cold War didn't really end — it paused. The arguments over its terms are still active and still lethal.

Importance & Impact

The Cold War didn't end cleanly. NATO expanded to 14 new members after 1991, which Russia framed as betrayal. Nuclear arsenals never went to zero. The INF Treaty collapsed in 2019. And the U.S. role as the lone global superpower from 1991 onward — with military deployments from Iraq to Afghanistan to the Pacific — is a direct result of how the Cold War ended. Every major foreign policy debate of the last three decades traces back to that moment.

Lesson 59 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

Moving Forward — Debating America's Founding Ideals

How does our nation define and debate progress as we work to preserve American ideals?

Three Examples
01 · Securing Liberty
First Amendment · Student Speech

Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L. (2021)

Decided
June 2021
Vote
8 – 1
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Justice Breyer

A high school cheerleader posted a profanity-filled Snapchat off campus over the weekend. Her school suspended her from the team. The Supreme Court ruled 8-1 that the school violated her First Amendment rights — the most important student speech ruling since Tinker v. Des Moines in 1969, and the first to directly address off-campus, online student speech.

What It Settled (and Didn't) Schools have "diminished" authority over off-campus speech but the Court didn't draw a bright line. Schools can still police off-campus cyberbullying. But a private social media post from home, outside school hours, is now substantially First Amendment protected. Tactic Used Strategic ACLU litigation, building on 50+ years of Tinker precedent, framed as a chilling effect on political speech.
02 · Safeguarding Equality
Title VII · Employment · LGBTQ+ Rights

Bostock v. Clayton County, Georgia (2020)

Decided
June 2020
Vote
6 – 3
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Justice Gorsuch

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits employment discrimination "because of sex." The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that this language also covers sexual orientation and gender identity. The majority opinion was written by Justice Gorsuch — a Trump appointee and committed textualist — applying a strict reading of what "because of sex" actually means.

Why It Mattered Before Bostock, LGBTQ+ employees in more than half of U.S. states had no explicit federal employment protection. The decision showed expanding equality can happen through reinterpretation of existing law, not just new legislation. Tactic Used Strategic case consolidation — three separate cases combined to maximize the chance of a single unified national ruling.
03 · Defending Democracy
Redistricting · Gerrymandering · Voting

Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) — and the State-Level Response

Decided
June 2019
Vote
5 – 4
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Chief Justice Roberts

The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that federal courts cannot hear partisan gerrymandering claims — they're "political questions." The ruling closed the federal door on challenges to maps drawn to entrench one party's power. But the opinion explicitly noted that state courts and state constitutions remained valid venues.

What Happened Next Since 2019, advocacy groups shifted to the state level: independent redistricting commissions in Michigan, Colorado, Arizona, and California; state supreme court challenges; and ballot initiatives. When one venue closes, the fight moves to another. Tactic Used Venue-shifting — from federal courts to state courts, ballot initiatives, and independent commission campaigns.
Modern Connection — ICED Method
I

In June 2021, the Supreme Court issued its first ruling specifically addressing off-campus, online student speech in Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L.

C

Writing for an 8-1 majority, Justice Breyer held that schools have "diminished" authority over off-campus student speech and that punishing the student's Snapchat post violated the First Amendment (Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L., 594 U.S. ___, 2021).

E

The ruling extended the 1969 Tinker v. Des Moines framework into the era of social media, partially protecting off-campus online speech while leaving room for schools to address cyberbullying and cheating.

D

For students today, this is the case that governs what you can post on Snapchat or Instagram without your school punishing you — the modern version of Mary Beth Tinker's black armband, just digital. Defending liberty isn't a one-time achievement; it gets re-litigated in every generation's medium.

Importance & Impact

The American ideals in the Declaration and the Constitution aren't self-enforcing. They get defended through specific tactics: strategic litigation, Supreme Court precedent-building, state-level workarounds, ballot initiatives, and grassroots pressure. Each generation re-fights for them in whatever venues are available. The fights look different now — Snapchat instead of armbands, redistricting commissions instead of Voting Rights Act lawsuits — but the underlying work is the same.
