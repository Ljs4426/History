Lesson 56 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

Ending the Cold War

What were the effects of Ronald Reagan's and George H. W. Bush's foreign policy actions?

Three Examples
01 · 1981–1987
Reagan Doctrine + Strategic Defense Initiative

Rejecting Détente: The Reagan Doctrine and "Star Wars"

Reagan rejected détente and went on offense. He funded anti-communist rebels in Nicaragua, Afghanistan, and Angola (the Reagan Doctrine) and pushed SDI (a space-based missile shield that didn't actually work yet) as a threat to force an arms race the Soviets couldn't afford.

Other Perspective One side credits Reagan's pressure with forcing the Soviet collapse. The other says Gorbachev's reforms and internal Soviet decay were already doing that. Reagan just happened to be president when it fell.
02 · 1985–1988
Geneva · Reykjavik · Washington · Moscow

Reagan-Gorbachev Summits and the INF Treaty

Reagan and Gorbachev met four times between 1985 and 1988: Geneva, Reykjavik, Washington, Moscow. The result was the INF Treaty in 1987, the first arms deal to actually eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons instead of just capping them. Over 2,600 missiles destroyed (Avalon Project, 1987). The Reykjavik summit in October 1986 came closest to something bigger — both leaders were hours away from agreeing to eliminate all ballistic missiles before the talks collapsed. The sticking point was SDI: Gorbachev wanted it confined to the lab for 10 years, Reagan refused to give it up. So the near-total-disarmament deal died and they settled for the INF instead, which was still historic but far less than what almost happened in Iceland.

Other Perspective Reykjavik almost produced a deal to eliminate all nuclear weapons, but it fell apart over SDI. The INF Treaty held for 30 years until the U.S. pulled out in 2019, restarting the arms competition it had paused.
03 · 1989–1991
Nov 9, 1989 · Dec 25, 1991

The Berlin Wall Falls and the USSR Dissolves

November 9, 1989: East Germany opened its borders, crowds tore down the Berlin Wall. December 25, 1991: the Soviet Union dissolved into 15 countries (TCI). Bush Sr. deliberately kept quiet (no victory speeches, no gloating) to avoid cornering Gorbachev and triggering a hardliner backlash that could've reversed everything.

Other Perspective Some Americans wanted a bigger celebration. Most historians now say the restraint was the right call. Humiliating Russia had consequences. When that restraint dropped off in later years — NATO expansion, Iraq — the relationship with Russia fell apart fast.
Modern Connection: ICED Method

Foreign policy scholar John Mearsheimer has argued that NATO's expansion eastward after the Cold War set up the current conflict with Russia. In a 2022 essay in Foreign Affairs, he wrote that U.S. policy after 1991 "ignored Russia's bright red lines" by pushing Ukraine toward NATO membership (Mearsheimer, Foreign Affairs, 2022). You can disagree with his argument, but the expansion decisions (Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic in 1999, then the Baltic states in 2004) built the security situation Russia has been reacting to. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine is evidence the Cold War didn't end, it just went quiet for a while. The disputes over who won and what that meant are still going.

Importance & Impact

The Cold War ended messy. NATO gained 14 new members. Nuclear arsenals never went to zero. The INF Treaty died in 2019. The U.S. became the world's only superpower and started showing up militarily everywhere: Iraq, Afghanistan, the Pacific. Every big foreign policy fight of the past 30 years starts from what got decided in 1991.
