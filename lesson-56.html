Lesson 56 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

Ending the Cold War

What were the effects of Ronald Reagan's and George H. W. Bush's foreign policy actions?

Three Examples
01 · 1981–1987
Reagan Doctrine + Strategic Defense Initiative

Rejecting Détente: The Reagan Doctrine and "Star Wars"

Reagan came into office rejecting the détente strategy of the Nixon and Carter years. His approach was more aggressive: support anti-communist fighters in Nicaragua, Afghanistan, and Angola through covert funding (the Reagan Doctrine), while pushing an arms buildup the Soviets couldn't match. The Strategic Defense Initiative — a proposed space-based missile shield — was technically unworkable at the time, but the threat of an arms race the USSR couldn't afford was real pressure.

POV Tension One school of historians credits Reagan's pressure with forcing the Soviet economic collapse that led to dissolution. Another argues Gorbachev's own reform program, internal Soviet decay, and nationalist movements in Eastern Europe were the real causes — and Reagan just got credit for a collapse that was already happening.
02 · 1985–1988
Geneva · Reykjavik · Washington · Moscow

Reagan-Gorbachev Summits and the INF Treaty

Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev held four summits between 1985 and 1988 — Geneva, Reykjavik, Washington, and Moscow. The result was the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty in 1987, the first arms control agreement that actually eliminated an entire class of nuclear weapons rather than just capping them. It destroyed over 2,600 missiles from both sides.

POV Tension The Reykjavik summit in 1986 nearly produced a full nuclear abolition agreement — Reagan and Gorbachev came close to agreeing to eliminate all ballistic missiles, but the deal fell apart over SDI. The INF Treaty held for three decades until the Trump administration withdrew in 2019, citing Russian violations, restarting the arms competition the treaty had paused.
03 · 1989–1991
Nov 9, 1989 · Dec 25, 1991

The Berlin Wall Falls and the USSR Dissolves

On November 9, 1989, East Germany opened its borders and crowds began tearing down the Berlin Wall. Two years later, on December 25, 1991, the Soviet Union formally dissolved into 15 independent states. President George H. W. Bush deliberately played a low-key role — no triumphal speeches, no victory laps — to avoid humiliating Gorbachev and triggering a hardliner coup that might have reversed the whole process.

POV Tension Bush's restraint was criticized at the time as too passive — some Americans wanted a celebration. Looking back, most historians credit the quiet diplomacy as the strategically correct call. A humiliated Russia lashing back against perceived Western triumphalism was a real risk, and that restraint (or lack of it in later years) shaped everything that followed.
Modern Connection — ICED Method
I

Foreign policy scholar John Mearsheimer has argued for over a decade that NATO's post-Cold War expansion eastward was the fundamental driver of current tensions between Russia and the West.

C

In a 2022 essay in Foreign Affairs, Mearsheimer wrote that U.S. policy after 1991 "ignored Russia's bright red lines" by encouraging Ukraine's aspirations to join NATO (Mearsheimer, "The Causes and Consequences of the Ukraine War," Foreign Affairs, 2022).

E

Whether or not you accept his full thesis, the core argument holds up: the choices Bush Sr. and his successors made about NATO between 1989 and 1999 — welcoming Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic in 1999, then the Baltic states in 2004 — set the security architecture that Russia has been reacting against ever since.

D

The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 and the war that's still ongoing demonstrate that the Cold War didn't really end — it paused. The arguments over its terms, over who "won" and what that entitled them to do, are still active and still lethal. That's a direct consequence of how the Cold War ended in 1989–1991.

Importance & Impact

The Cold War didn't end cleanly. The Soviet Union dissolved, but the borders, alliances, and grievances that came out of that ending shape the world today. NATO expanded to 14 new members after 1991, mostly from the former Soviet sphere, which Russia framed as a betrayal of post-Cold War understandings. Nuclear arsenals never went to zero — the INF Treaty collapsed in 2019, and arms control infrastructure is weaker now than at any point since the 1970s. And the U.S. role as the lone global superpower from 1991 onward — with military deployments from Iraq to Afghanistan to the Pacific — is a direct result of how the Cold War ended. Every major foreign policy debate of the last three decades traces back to that moment.
