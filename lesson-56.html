Lesson 56 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

Ending the Cold War

What were the effects of Ronald Reagan's and George H. W. Bush's foreign policy actions?

Three Examples
01 · 1981–1987
Reagan Doctrine + Strategic Defense Initiative

Rejecting Détente: The Reagan Doctrine and "Star Wars"

Reagan rejected détente and went on offense. He funded anti-communist rebels in Nicaragua, Afghanistan, and Angola (the Reagan Doctrine) and pushed SDI — a space-based missile shield that didn't actually work yet — as a threat to force an arms race the Soviets couldn't afford.

POV Tension One side credits Reagan's pressure with forcing the Soviet collapse. The other says Gorbachev's reforms and internal Soviet decay were already doing that — Reagan just happened to be president when it fell.
02 · 1985–1988
Geneva · Reykjavik · Washington · Moscow

Reagan-Gorbachev Summits and the INF Treaty

Reagan and Gorbachev met four times between 1985 and 1988: Geneva, Reykjavik, Washington, Moscow. The result was the INF Treaty in 1987 — the first arms deal to actually eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons instead of just capping them. Over 2,600 missiles destroyed.

POV Tension Reykjavik almost produced a deal to eliminate all nuclear weapons — it fell apart over SDI. The INF Treaty held for 30 years until the U.S. pulled out in 2019, restarting the arms competition it had paused.
03 · 1989–1991
Nov 9, 1989 · Dec 25, 1991

The Berlin Wall Falls and the USSR Dissolves

November 9, 1989: East Germany opened its borders, crowds tore down the Berlin Wall. December 25, 1991: the Soviet Union dissolved into 15 countries. Bush Sr. deliberately kept quiet — no victory speeches, no gloating — to avoid cornering Gorbachev and triggering a hardliner backlash that could've reversed everything.

POV Tension Some Americans wanted a bigger celebration. Most historians now say the restraint was the right call. Humiliating Russia had consequences — the lack of that restraint in later years shaped everything that followed.
Modern Connection — ICED Method
I

Foreign policy scholar John Mearsheimer has argued that NATO's eastward expansion after the Cold War was the main driver of current tensions between Russia and the West.

C

In a 2022 essay in Foreign Affairs, Mearsheimer wrote that U.S. policy after 1991 "ignored Russia's bright red lines" by encouraging Ukraine's aspirations to join NATO (Mearsheimer, "The Causes and Consequences of the Ukraine War," Foreign Affairs, 2022).

E

Whether or not you buy his full argument, the NATO expansion decisions — Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic in 1999, then the Baltic states in 2004 — set up the security situation Russia has been reacting against ever since.

D

Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine shows the Cold War didn't end — it paused. The arguments about who "won" and what they were entitled to do afterward are still active and still lethal. That's a direct result of how 1989–1991 played out.

Importance & Impact

The Cold War ended messy. NATO gained 14 new members. Nuclear arsenals never went to zero — the INF Treaty died in 2019. The U.S. became the world's only superpower and started showing up militarily everywhere: Iraq, Afghanistan, the Pacific. Every major foreign policy debate of the past 30 years traces back to how 1991 played out.
