## Home Page

### Intro Paragraph
From the Reagan Revolution to the fall of the Berlin Wall to landmark Supreme Court rulings, Unit 8 covers the forces that reshaped America from the early 1980s to right now. This site digs into three lessons: how Reagan's economic and foreign policies changed what Americans expect from government, how the Cold War ended and why that ending still drives global conflict today, and how the ideals in the Constitution get defended — and re-defended — in every generation. The past isn't far away. Most of these fights are still going on.

## Lesson 55 Page — A Shift to the Right Under Reagan

### Page Title
Lesson 55: A Shift to the Right Under Reagan

### Lesson Question
How did the Reagan Revolution impact the nation?

### Intro Paragraph
When Ronald Reagan took office in January 1981, he came in with a specific economic program: cut taxes, reduce regulation, build up the military, and trust the market to sort out the rest. The "Reagan Revolution" wasn't just a set of policies — it was a shift in what Americans believed government was supposed to do. Whether you think it worked depends on which Americans you're looking at. This exhibit walks through three major examples of how Reagan's policies changed the country, what the debate looked like then, and how those decisions are still showing up in headlines today.

## Lesson 56 Page — Ending the Cold War

### Page Title
Lesson 56: Ending the Cold War

### Lesson Question
What were the effects of Ronald Reagan's and George H. W. Bush's foreign policy actions?

### Intro Paragraph
The Cold War was a 45-year standoff between the United States and the Soviet Union that shaped everything from military spending to school curriculum to how American politicians talked about freedom. It ended faster than almost anyone predicted — and the way it ended matters as much as the fact that it ended. Reagan's aggressive posture toward the USSR set the stage, Gorbachev's reforms created the opening, and Bush Sr.'s careful diplomacy managed the collapse. But the map that came out of that ending — the alliances, the grievances, the unanswered questions — is the map the world is still living with.

## Lesson 59 Page — Debating America's Founding Ideals

### Page Title
Lesson 59: Moving Forward — Debating America's Founding Ideals

### Lesson Question
How does our nation define and debate progress as we work to preserve American ideals?

### Intro Paragraph
The ideals in the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution — liberty, equality, democracy — aren't self-enforcing. Every generation has to re-fight for them, using the tools and venues available at the time. In the 1960s it was marches and legislation. Today it's strategic Supreme Court litigation, state-level redistricting commissions, and social media cases that end up in front of nine justices. Three recent cases show what that looks like right now: a cheerleader's Snapchat post, a funeral home's firing policy, and a gerrymandered congressional map. The tactics are new. The ideals aren't.
