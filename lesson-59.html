Lesson 59 Unit 8 · Pursuing American Ideals

Moving Forward — Debating America's Founding Ideals

How does our nation define and debate progress as we work to preserve American ideals?

Three Examples
01 · Securing Liberty
First Amendment · Student Speech

Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L. (2021)

Decided
June 2021
Vote
8 – 1
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Justice Breyer

A high school cheerleader in Pennsylvania didn't make varsity and posted a profanity-filled Snapchat from off campus over the weekend. Her school suspended her from the team. She — and the ACLU — sued. The Supreme Court ruled 8-1 that the school violated her First Amendment rights. It's the most important student speech ruling since Tinker v. Des Moines in 1969, and the first time the Court directly addressed off-campus, online student speech.

What It Settled (and Didn't) The Court said schools have "diminished" authority over off-campus speech but didn't draw a bright line. Schools can still police off-campus cyberbullying and academic dishonesty. But a student's private social media post from home, outside school hours, is now substantially protected by the First Amendment. The Tinker armband is now a Snapchat. Tactic Used Strategic ACLU litigation, building on 50+ years of Tinker precedent, framed as a chilling effect on political speech.
02 · Safeguarding Equality
Title VII · Employment · LGBTQ+ Rights

Bostock v. Clayton County, Georgia (2020)

Decided
June 2020
Vote
6 – 3
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Justice Gorsuch

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits employment discrimination "because of sex." In Bostock, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that this language also protects employees from discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity. The majority opinion was written by Justice Gorsuch — a Trump appointee and one of the Court's most committed textualists — who applied a strict reading of what "because of sex" means.

Why It Mattered Before Bostock, LGBTQ+ employees in more than half of U.S. states had no explicit federal employment protection. After Bostock, they did — through a statute written in 1964, reinterpreted without any new legislation. The decision showed that expanding equality protections doesn't always require Congress to act; it can happen through courts reinterpreting existing law. Tactic Used Strategic case consolidation — three separate cases (Bostock, Altitude Express, R.G. & G.R. Harris Funeral Homes) combined to maximize the chance of a single unified national ruling.
03 · Defending Democracy
Redistricting · Gerrymandering · Voting

Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) — and the State-Level Response

Decided
June 2019
Vote
5 – 4
Court
U.S. Supreme Court
Author
Chief Justice Roberts

The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in Rucho that federal courts cannot hear claims of partisan gerrymandering — they're "political questions" beyond the judiciary's reach. The ruling closed the federal door on challenges to maps drawn to entrench one party's power, even when the maps are extreme. But the decision explicitly noted that state courts and state constitutions remained valid venues.

What Happened Next Since 2019, advocacy groups shifted strategy to the state level: independent redistricting commissions in Michigan, Colorado, Arizona, and California; state supreme court rulings in Pennsylvania and North Carolina striking down partisan maps; and ballot initiatives bypassing legislatures entirely. When one venue closes, the fight moves to another. Tactic Used Venue-shifting — from federal courts to state courts, ballot initiatives, and independent commission campaigns after the federal door closed.
Modern Connection — ICED Method
I

In June 2021, the Supreme Court issued its first ruling specifically addressing off-campus, online student speech in Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L. — a case that started with a cheerleader's expletive-filled Snapchat story sent from a convenience store on a Saturday.

C

Writing for an 8-1 majority, Justice Breyer held that schools have "diminished" authority over off-campus student speech and that punishing the student's Snapchat post violated the First Amendment (Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L., 594 U.S. ___, 2021).

E

The ruling extended the 1969 Tinker v. Des Moines framework — which held that students don't "shed their constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate" — into the era of social media, partially protecting off-campus online speech while leaving room for schools to address cyberbullying and cheating.

D

For students today, this is the case that governs what you can post on Snapchat, Instagram, or TikTok without your school punishing you for it. It's the modern version of Mary Beth Tinker's black armband, just digital and with a lot more reach — and it shows that defending liberty isn't a one-time achievement; it gets re-litigated in every generation's medium.

Importance & Impact

The American ideals in the Declaration and the Constitution aren't self-enforcing. They get defended through specific tactics: strategic litigation, Supreme Court precedent-building, state-level workarounds, ballot initiatives, advocacy coalitions, and grassroots pressure. Each generation re-fights for them in whatever venues are available. The fights look different now — Snapchat instead of armbands, redistricting commissions instead of Voting Rights Act lawsuits — but the underlying work is the same. Three cases from 2019 to 2021 added new chapters to that story, and the chapters being written right now are just as consequential.
